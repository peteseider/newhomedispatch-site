Why a rate-buydown promotion costs the builder real money — and how that can work for you

When a builder advertises a permanently lower rate, it isn't the builder lending you money directly — it's the builder paying its mortgage partner a lump sum, upfront, to buy that rate down for the life of your loan. That lump sum, called a forward-commitment or discount-point fee, typically runs about 4–6% of the loan amount. On a $400,000 loan, that's roughly $16,000–$24,000 the builder is paying someone else so your rate looks better in the ad.

That number matters because it's real money changing hands on your purchase — and builders will often let you reallocate it. If you don't need the rate buydown (say you're paying cash, refinancing soon, or your own lender already beats the builder's rate), you can frequently ask the builder to apply that same budget to the purchase price instead, or to closing costs, upgrades, or other credits. Builders don't always offer this up front, because the advertised rate is the version that moves the most inventory — but the underlying dollars are usually negotiable once you know they exist.

Today's Regency by Toll Brothers rate is still the only candidate we've confirmed in over a week — and it remains independently uncorroborated after three straight runs. Before assuming the advertised 5.49% is the full story, ask the builder directly for the forward-commitment fee behind it, and ask what that budget would be worth to you as cash or closing-cost credit instead.

New construction is now cheaper than resale in a growing number of markets, reporting finds

Recent reporting highlights a structural shift: the long-standing price premium new homes have historically carried over comparable resale listings has narrowed or inverted in a number of U.S. metros, driven by builders' sustained use of rate buydowns, price cuts, and closing-cost credits to move inventory. (FOX 7 Austin, published Oct 4, 2026 — now in its 9th day in this slot; we checked two fresher candidates this run, including a Community Impact piece on a Georgetown builder that turned out to be paid sponsored content credited to the builder itself, and an independent but 14-months-stale piece on a different Georgetown community — neither cleared our bar, so it stays.)

For buyers: this is exactly the mechanism we track here every day. Austin-area builders are carrying a median advertised incentive around $30,000 across 34 active builders today, with several specific communities exceeding $80,000–$105,000. Before assuming a resale listing is the cheaper option, get the builder's current incentive-adjusted price in writing and run the full comparison — price, incentive value, HOA, warranty, and any lender-tied rate terms — rather than comparing list prices alone.
