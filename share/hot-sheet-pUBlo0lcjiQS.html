What a permanent rate buydown actually costs — and how to use that

A permanent below-market rate isn't free. To hold, say, 4.99% when the market is 6.67%, the builder pre-pays the lender a forward-commitment fee that commonly runs about 4–6% of the loan amount — roughly $18,000–$27,000 on a $450,000 loan. That is real builder money, and it is the same pool of money a builder could otherwise hand you as a price cut or closing-cost credit.

So the rate is a choice, not a gift. If you plan to keep the loan a long time, the permanent buydown usually wins. But if you're paying cash, expect to move or refinance within a few years, or simply value price over payment, ask the builder to reallocate that buydown cost to the sale price or to closing costs instead — several builders here (Perry, Stylecraft) already frame it as "rate OR cash, your choice."

One more check: nearly every rate on this sheet requires the builder's affiliated lender. That lender can quietly offset the incentive through a higher price or fees. Always put the affiliated lender's Loan Estimate next to an outside lender's before you sign.

New construction is underpricing resale in Austin Metro right now

Spectrum News 1 Texas reports Austin-area buyers are increasingly choosing new construction over resale as builders lean on incentives to move inventory. The median price for newly built homes in Austin Metro was about $411,000 in July, versus $435,000 for all homes listed area-wide — new construction is running roughly $24,000 cheaper at the median. Some communities are advertising first-year mortgage rates as low as 3.25% against a prevailing market rate around 6.625%, plus design credits and closing-cost assistance. Austin realtor Justyn LeFebvre is quoted saying resale sellers increasingly have to price "more in line with where the builders are building." For buyers: that $24,000 median gap is a structural condition right now, not a one-off promotion — if you're cross-shopping new vs. resale, compare total cost (price minus incentive value, including the true cost of a temporary buydown) rather than list price alone, and ask every builder whether an advertised first-year rate is permanent or reverts after year one.
